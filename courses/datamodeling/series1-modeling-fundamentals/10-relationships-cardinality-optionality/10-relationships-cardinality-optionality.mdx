## Every line starts as a sentence

The last lesson found the **things** a business keeps track of. This one draws the **lines between them**, and every line starts as a sentence about the business:

> **Every order is placed by exactly one customer. A customer may have placed none.**

Two sentences, one line. Watch what the words **exactly one** and **may** turn into.

## Three marks

![The three marks of crow's-foot notation: a bar means one, a circle means zero (may be missing), a crow's foot means many; nearer the box is the most, further out is the least. Beside them the four line ends they make, each drawn against a box: two bars mean exactly one; a circle then a bar mean zero or one; a bar then a crow's foot mean one or many; a circle then a crow's foot mean zero or many.](/images/academy/datamodeling-series1-10/crows-foot.svg)

Crow's-foot notation has three marks:

- a **bar** means **one**,
- a **circle** means **zero**: the thing is allowed to be missing,
- a **crow's foot** means **many**.

Each end of a line carries two marks, and says two things. The mark nearer the box is **the most** there can be, called the **cardinality**. The mark further out is **the least**, called the **optionality**.

## The sentence, drawn

![Customer and Order joined by one line. At Customer, two bars labelled exactly one. At Order, a circle and a crow's foot labelled zero or many, with the circle ringed. Under the diagram, the line read back: "Every order is placed by exactly one customer. A customer may have placed none, or many."](/images/academy/datamodeling-series1-10/customer-order.svg)

At `Customer`: two bars, **exactly one**. At `Order`: a circle and a crow's foot, **zero or many**.

Read it back out loud: *every order has exactly one customer, and a customer has zero or many orders*. You get the sentence back. That circle at the order end is what the next two queries put to the test.

## What does the data say?

Count each customer's orders, fewest first. If a customer can have none, the data should show one:

```sql file=./scripts/fewest-orders.sql
SELECT "CustomerID",
       count(*) AS "Orders"
FROM "Orders"
GROUP BY "CustomerID"
ORDER BY "Orders", "CustomerID"
LIMIT 3;
```

| CustomerID | Orders |
| ---------- | ------ |
| QUICK      | 2      |
| TOMSP      | 2      |
| WANDK      | 2      |

The fewest is 2. And a `LEFT JOIN` confirms that no customer is without an order:

```sql file=./scripts/customers-without-orders.sql
SELECT count(*) AS "Customers without orders"
FROM "Customers" c
LEFT JOIN "Orders" o
  ON o."CustomerID" = c."CustomerID"
WHERE o."OrderID" IS NULL;
```

| Customers without orders |
| ------------------------ |
| 0                        |

So the data says every customer has orders. Should the circle go, making the end **one or many**?

## What happened, or what is allowed?

Picture a customer who signed up this morning. How many orders have they placed? **None.** That customer is perfectly real, and the business allows them. The data simply does not have them yet.

> **The data shows what happened. The model says what is allowed.**

The data can prove a line is **too strict**: one real row that breaks a rule is enough. It can never prove that an end is **not** optional, because the row that would need the circle may not exist yet. Keep the circle.

## Products and suppliers

The same test on a second line. Every product has exactly one supplier. How many products does each supplier have?

```sql file=./scripts/products-per-supplier.sql
SELECT s."CompanyName",
       count(*) AS "Products"
FROM "Suppliers" s
JOIN "Products" p
  ON p."SupplierID" = s."SupplierID"
GROUP BY s."SupplierID", s."CompanyName"
ORDER BY s."SupplierID";
```

## Is the shipper optional?

```sql file=./scripts/unshipped-orders.sql
SELECT count(*) AS "Unshipped",
       count("ShipVia") AS "With a shipper"
FROM "Orders"
WHERE "ShippedDate" IS NULL;
```

| Unshipped | With a shipper |
| --------- | -------------- |
| 27        | 27             |

27 orders have not shipped yet, and all 27 already name a shipper. Is the shipper mandatory, then?

It depends on the business, not on these rows. If this business picks the shipper when the order is placed, every order has one: **exactly one**. If another business picks the shipper only when the order actually ships, an order can wait without one: **zero or one**. Same data today, two different line ends. **Ask before you draw the circle.**

## A line can have a name

The column in `"Orders"` that points at `"Shippers"` is not called `"ShipperID"`. It is called `"ShipVia"`. The name says the **role** the shipper plays for the order: the line *ships via*.

Role names matter as soon as one table points at another more than once. An order could name a shipper that carries it **and** a different shipper that returns it: two lines between the same two tables, told apart only by their roles.

## A line back to itself

```sql file=./scripts/reports-to.sql
SELECT e."FirstName" AS "Employee",
       m."FirstName" AS "Reports to"
FROM "Employees" e
LEFT JOIN "Employees" m
  ON m."EmployeeID" = e."ReportsTo"
ORDER BY e."EmployeeID";
```

## One line, many at both ends

```sql file=./scripts/products-on-order-1.sql
SELECT "OrderID", "ProductID"
FROM "Order Details"
WHERE "OrderID" = 1
ORDER BY "ProductID";
```

| OrderID | ProductID |
| ------- | --------- |
| 1       | 1         |
| 1       | 6         |

One order holds many products. And one product sits on many orders:

```sql file=./scripts/orders-with-chai.sql
SELECT count(*) AS "Orders with Chai"
FROM "Order Details"
WHERE "ProductID" = 1;
```

| Orders with Chai |
| ---------------- |
| 10               |

That is **many at both ends**: a crow's foot on both sides of one line, called **many-to-many**. Where such a line actually goes in a database has a lesson of its own.

## Write the diagram as text

In CloudBeaver's **ER Diagram** tab (`er-diagram`) you do not have to draw with a mouse. You can write the diagram as **PlantUML** text, and text can be compared, reviewed, changed and kept:

```
@startuml
entity Customer
entity Order
entity Supplier
entity Product
entity Shipper
entity Employee
Customer ||--o{ Order : places
Supplier ||--o{ Product : supplies
Shipper |o--o{ Order : "ships via"
Employee |o--o{ Employee : "reports to"
@enduml
```

The symbols are the marks: `||` exactly one, `|o` zero or one, `o{` zero or many, `|{` one or many. `Customer ||--o{ Order` is the sentence from the top of this page in five symbols.

## Read it back

Reading a line out loud is how you catch a wrong one. Try these three:

1. `Customer ||--o{ Order`: every order has exactly one customer; a customer has zero or many orders. **Right.**
2. `Supplier ||--|{ Product`: every supplier has **one or more** products. **Wrong.** The business said a new supplier may have none, so that end needs a circle: `o{`.
3. `Employee |o--o{ Employee`: an employee has zero or one manager; a manager has zero or many reports. **Right.**

Say the line, and listen for a sentence the business would not say.

## Northwind declares none of this

One honest note. Northwind **declares none of these lines**: the database does not know about a single one. The diagram above was **inferred**, from column names, from the data and from sentences about the business. Making the database enforce these lines has a lesson of its own.

## Sentence first

> **Say the sentence. Then draw the line. The data never decides the circle.**

A row that breaks a rule shows up in the data. A customer who might exist tomorrow shows up only in the business.
