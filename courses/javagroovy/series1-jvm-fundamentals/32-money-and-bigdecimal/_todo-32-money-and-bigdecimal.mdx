## See it for yourself

{/* BEAT 3 — NON-NEGOTIABLE. The koan above, or the reason there isn't one.
    If this beat cannot be written, the episode is not ready to be written.

    predict: the rounding boundary, three values, before running anything.
        1.005  ->  Math.round(v*100)/100.0 = 1.00   BigDecimal HALF_UP = 1.01
        1.015  ->                            1.01                       1.02
        2.675  ->                            2.68                       2.68
    THE THIRD LINE IS WHY THIS IS AN EPISODE. The double is not reliably low, it is
    UNPREDICTABLE — whether the binary value lands just under or just over the half is a
    property of that particular number. "Doubles round down" would be a false rule. There
    is no rule, and that is the finding.

    diagnose: the reconciliation check that can never pass. 0.1 + 0.2 is
    0.30000000000000004, so (0.1 + 0.2) == 0.3 is FALSE. Every job in Series 2 and 3 ends
    by proving the number it loaded equals the number it read. Written against doubles that
    check either never goes green, or gets "fixed" with a tolerance — and a tolerance wide
    enough to swallow binary error is wide enough to swallow a missing row. */}

    Every episode in this track earns its place by preventing a wrong number or a

    WHAT IS NOT THE LESSON, and say so rather than letting the reader assume it:
    ACCUMULATION DRIFT IS REAL BUT TINY. Ten thousand lines of 0.07 sum to 700.000000000091;
    a thousand lines of 19.99 to 19990.000000000135. Both wrong, neither visible on a report
    rounded to two places. Building the episode on "your totals drift" would teach a failure
    the reader will never see.

    THE CONSTRUCTS, all verified:
      new BigDecimal(0.1)     -> 0.1000000000000000055511151231257827021181583404541015625
      BigDecimal.valueOf(0.1) -> 0.1
        The first takes the double's error as its exact value, and is the most common way a
        codebase that "uses BigDecimal" is still wrong.
      new BigDecimal("2.0").equals(new BigDecimal("2.00")) -> FALSE;  compareTo -> 0
        Scale is part of identity, not of value, so a test asserting a CORRECT total fails.
        Also where the header's one Groovy warning bites: Groovy's == is equals().
      BigDecimal.ONE.divide(new BigDecimal("3")) -> ArithmeticException, "Non-terminating
        decimal expansion; no exact representable decimal result". Division is the operation
        that forces you to choose a scale and a RoundingMode. With (2, HALF_UP): 0.33

    BIGDECIMAL DOES NOT SAVE YOU FROM THE INVOICE THAT WILL NOT FOOT, and the episode must
    say so or it becomes "use BigDecimal and stop worrying". Three lines of 10.10, 20.20 and
    30.30 at 8.5% tax: sum of the rounded lines is 65.76, rounded sum is 65.75 — IN BOTH
    TYPES. That is not a numeric-type bug, it is a decision about WHERE rounding happens.
    Every finance system makes it explicitly. END HERE: pick the place, write it down, and
    reconcile against it.

    LOCALE, found by accident while verifying the above: String.format("%.2f", 1.0) printed
    "1,00" on the machine this was run on, because %f follows the DEFAULT LOCALE. The same
    job writes 1.00 on the build server and 1,00 on a European workstation, breaking the CSV
    downstream and shifting a column. BigDecimal.toString() is locale-independent. Straight
    back to episode 20.
