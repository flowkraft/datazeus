    ── WHAT THIS LESSON SHOULD COVER (from curriculum.yaml) ────────────────
     COVER: HA, and then the sentence this course exists partly to deliver.
     WHAT IT DOES: the HA manager watches resources; when a node stops answering, its VMs are
     started on another node. FENCING is what stops the old node writing to the same disks —
     a hardware watchdog reboots a node that has lost quorum. Without fencing you get split
     brain, which on shared storage means two machines writing to one filesystem, which means
     the data is gone.
     THE REQUIREMENTS: three nodes or a QDevice, shared storage or replication, and a
     watchdog that actually works. HA on two nodes with no QDevice is a way to lose data.
     ⚠ AND THE MISUNDERSTANDING, STATED AS PLAINLY AS THIS COURSE CAN MANAGE:
       HA OF A VM IS NOT HA OF A DATABASE. What HA gives you is: the machine comes back
       somewhere else, in a few minutes, having been powered off without warning. Your
       database then does crash recovery, replays its write-ahead log, and comes up. Every
       connection was dropped. Every in-flight transaction was rolled back. Anything that was
       only in memory is gone.
       THAT IS GENUINELY USEFUL AND IT IS NOT WHAT A DATABASE PERSON MEANS BY THE WORDS.
       Real database availability is streaming replication and a failover decision, which is
       Data Ops, Series 2 · 45 — a DIFFERENT MECHANISM AT A DIFFERENT LAYER, and the two are
       complementary rather than alternatives.
     THE HONEST QUESTION TO CLOSE ON: what is your actual recovery time objective, and would
     a documented ten-minute manual restart have met it? Very often it would, and HA's
     complexity is then a cost with no matching benefit.
