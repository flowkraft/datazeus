## Two things a join does without a word

In the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/select-sum-join-group-by-order-by-limit-project" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">last lesson of Series 1</a>, a join multiplied the rows of a report, and `count(DISTINCT …)` put the count right. This lesson is about the two cases that lesson left out, because they are harder to see:

1. **A join that drops rows** that a `LEFT JOIN` was supposed to keep.
2. **A join that multiplies money**: a total that is too big, with nothing in the result to show it.

Neither one produces an error. Both are about the same question: **one row of this result is one what?** At the end, the same tool answers the next question of this series' case: how much money is on the orders that were marked shipped and never invoiced.

Every query below runs on Northwind Company, the dataset installed in the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/subqueries" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">first lesson of this series</a>, and assumes `SET search_path TO northwind_co_s;` has been run in the CloudBeaver editor. Write each query and compare the result with the table under it.

## Every Dairy product, and its May units

The first report comes from the manager of the Dairy Products category (category 4): **every Dairy product, with the units it sold in May 2024**, including the products that sold nothing, because those are the ones she wants to talk about. "Every product" means a `LEFT JOIN` from `"Products"`, as in the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/joins" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">JOINs lesson</a>, and May is a half-open range, as in the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/dates-and-times" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">dates lesson</a>. The May test goes in the `ON`:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the two joins, in grey. */}

```sql file=./scripts/dairy-units-left-then-inner.sql {4,6}
SELECT p."ProductName",
       sum(d."Quantity") AS "May units"
FROM "Products" p
LEFT JOIN "Order Details" d
  ON d."ProductID" = p."ProductID"
JOIN "Orders" o
  ON o."OrderID" = d."OrderID"
 AND o."OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-05-01'
 AND o."OrderDate" <  DATE '2024-06-01'
WHERE p."CategoryID" = 4
GROUP BY p."ProductName"
ORDER BY p."ProductName";
```

| ProductName      | May units |
| ---------------- | --------- |
| Coastal Parmesan | 10        |
| Harvest Butter   | 408       |
| Heritage Butter  | 836       |
| Heritage Gouda   | 58        |
| Highland Yoghurt | 52        |
| Rustic Butter    | 471       |
| Smoked Feta      | 56        |

**Count the rows before and after.** Ten Dairy products went in; seven rows came out. The query still says `LEFT JOIN`, and three products are missing, which are exactly the three the manager asked about.

The `LEFT JOIN` to `"Order Details"` kept every product. Then the plain `JOIN` to `"Orders"` kept only the rows that matched a May order, and removed every other row, including the ones the `LEFT JOIN` had kept. **An inner join after a left join drops what the left join kept.** It is the ON-versus-WHERE problem from the JOINs lesson, one table further along.

Here the rows went missing because the match was narrowed to one month: over five years, every product in Northwind Company has sold. Rows with no match at all go the same way. One customer, Yarrow Supermarket, has never placed an order, and three people in `"Employees"` (the chief executive and the two sales managers) take no orders, so an inner join to `"Orders"` leaves them out of any customer or employee report.

## LEFT all the way: it looks repaired

The obvious fix is to make the second join a `LEFT JOIN` too:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the second LEFT JOIN, in grey. */}

```sql file=./scripts/dairy-units-left-all-the-way.sql {6}
SELECT p."ProductName",
       sum(d."Quantity") AS "May units"
FROM "Products" p
LEFT JOIN "Order Details" d
  ON d."ProductID" = p."ProductID"
LEFT JOIN "Orders" o
  ON o."OrderID" = d."OrderID"
 AND o."OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-05-01'
 AND o."OrderDate" <  DATE '2024-06-01'
WHERE p."CategoryID" = 4
GROUP BY p."ProductName"
ORDER BY p."ProductName";
```

## Narrow first, then join

The fix is to answer the May question first, in brackets, and then join the products to the answer. The brackets add up May's lines per product — a table in `FROM`, from the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/subqueries" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">subqueries lesson</a> — and the products are `LEFT JOIN`ed to that table:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the May range, in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/dairy-units-narrowed-first.sql {10-11}#good
SELECT p."ProductName",
       m."May units"
FROM "Products" p
LEFT JOIN (SELECT d."ProductID",
                  sum(d."Quantity")
                    AS "May units"
           FROM "Order Details" d
           JOIN "Orders" o
             ON o."OrderID" = d."OrderID"
           WHERE o."OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-05-01'
             AND o."OrderDate" <  DATE '2024-06-01'
           GROUP BY d."ProductID") AS m
  ON m."ProductID" = p."ProductID"
WHERE p."CategoryID" = 4
ORDER BY p."ProductName";
```

## A join that multiplies money

The next request: **sales and freight per customer for May 2024**, on one report, top five by freight. It is the customer report from the end of Series 1 with one more `SUM`:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the freight line, in grey. */}

```sql file=./scripts/sales-and-freight-per-customer.sql {4}
SELECT c."CompanyName",
       ROUND(SUM(d."UnitPrice" * d."Quantity"
         * (1 - d."Discount")), 2) AS "Total sales",
       SUM(o."Freight") AS "Freight"
FROM "Customers" c
JOIN "Orders" o
  ON o."CustomerID" = c."CustomerID"
JOIN "Order Details" d
  ON d."OrderID" = o."OrderID"
WHERE o."OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-05-01'
  AND o."OrderDate" <  DATE '2024-06-01'
GROUP BY c."CompanyName"
ORDER BY "Freight" DESC
LIMIT 5;
```

### Why the freight grew

Look at Nordic Foods' May orders and how many lines each one has:

```sql file=./scripts/nordic-foods-orders-and-lines.sql
SELECT o."OrderID", o."Freight",
       count(*) AS "Lines"
FROM "Orders" o
JOIN "Order Details" d
  ON d."OrderID" = o."OrderID"
WHERE o."CustomerID" = 'NORDI'
  AND o."OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-05-01'
  AND o."OrderDate" <  DATE '2024-06-01'
GROUP BY o."OrderID", o."Freight"
ORDER BY o."OrderID";
```

| OrderID | Freight | Lines |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| 8374    | 184.43  | 5     |
| 8468    | 59.69   | 3     |
| 8482    | 44.27   | 2     |
| 8548    | 191.9   | 5     |

`"Freight"` is stored once per order. After joining `"Order Details"`, one row is one **order line**, and each line carries a copy of its order's freight. `SUM` adds every copy: 184.43 × 5 + 59.69 × 3 + 44.27 × 2 + 191.9 × 5 = 2149.26. An order with more lines gets its freight counted more times, so four big orders overtook Alpine Provisions' thirteen small ones.

Across the whole company, all five years:

```sql file=./scripts/company-freight-two-ways.sql
SELECT (SELECT SUM("Freight")
        FROM "Orders") AS "Orders alone",
       (SELECT SUM(o."Freight")
        FROM "Orders" o
        JOIN "Order Details" d
          ON d."OrderID" = o."OrderID")
         AS "After the join";
```

| Orders alone | After the join |
| ------------ | -------------- |
| 527429.46    | 1712919.25     |

The joined total is more than three times the real freight bill, and nothing reported a problem. The sales column in the first report was correct all along: sales are calculated from the lines, and one row is one line.

## SUM(DISTINCT) is not a fix

Series 1 fixed a multiplied count with `DISTINCT`, so it is tempting to write `SUM(DISTINCT o."Freight")`:

{/* The same highlight as the video: SUM(DISTINCT) in red. */}

```sql file=./scripts/sum-distinct-freight.sql {1}#err
SELECT SUM(DISTINCT o."Freight")
         AS "SUM(DISTINCT)"
FROM "Orders" o
JOIN "Order Details" d
  ON d."OrderID" = o."OrderID";
```

| SUM(DISTINCT) |
| ------------- |
| 391726.53     |

It is smaller than the joined total, but it is not the freight bill either: it is 135702.93 short of 527429.46. `SUM(DISTINCT …)` adds each distinct **value** once. It does not know about orders. When two orders have the same freight, it counts that value once and loses the other order. In Northwind Company that happens all the time:

```sql file=./scripts/orders-and-freight-values.sql
SELECT count(*) AS "Orders",
       count(DISTINCT "Freight")
         AS "Freight values"
FROM "Orders";
```

| Orders | Freight values |
| ------ | -------------- |
| 10000  | 6689           |

Ten thousand orders share 6689 different freight values. On a smaller slice the error is smaller and harder to spot: for May 2024 alone, `SUM(DISTINCT)` returns 8837.47 against a real 8977.88, because 181 orders have 178 different values. A number that is only slightly wrong is the one nobody checks.

## Aggregate first, then join

The correct version adds up each measure where one row is one of its own, and joins the results afterwards: sales per customer from the order lines, freight per customer from `"Orders"` alone, each in its own brackets.

{/* The same highlight as the video: the freight brackets, in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/sales-and-freight-aggregated-first.sql {13-18}#good
SELECT c."CompanyName", s."Total sales", f."Freight"
FROM "Customers" c
JOIN (SELECT o."CustomerID",
             ROUND(SUM(d."UnitPrice" * d."Quantity"
               * (1 - d."Discount")), 2) AS "Total sales"
      FROM "Orders" o
      JOIN "Order Details" d
        ON d."OrderID" = o."OrderID"
      WHERE o."OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-05-01'
        AND o."OrderDate" <  DATE '2024-06-01'
      GROUP BY o."CustomerID") AS s
  ON s."CustomerID" = c."CustomerID"
JOIN (SELECT "CustomerID",
             SUM("Freight") AS "Freight"
      FROM "Orders"
      WHERE "OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-05-01'
        AND "OrderDate" <  DATE '2024-06-01'
      GROUP BY "CustomerID") AS f
  ON f."CustomerID" = c."CustomerID"
ORDER BY f."Freight" DESC
LIMIT 5;
```

## One row is one what?

Before adding up a column after a join, ask the question from the end of Series 1: **one row of this result is one what?** After joining the order lines, one row is one order line. Freight belongs to one order, so it can only be summed where one row is one order. Sales belong to one line, so they can be summed over the lines. This is called the **grain** of a table or a result, and the rule is:

**Sum each measure at its own grain, then join the results.**

### A bridge table

`"Order Details"` is a **bridge** table: it connects orders and products, which have a many-to-many relationship. Through a bridge, `count(*)` counts lines. Count something else only when you say so:

```sql file=./scripts/heritage-butter-lines-and-customers.sql
SELECT count(*) AS "Lines",
       count(DISTINCT o."CustomerID")
         AS "Customers"
FROM "Order Details" d
JOIN "Orders" o
  ON o."OrderID" = d."OrderID"
WHERE d."ProductID" = 28;
```

| Lines | Customers |
| ----- | --------- |
| 779   | 78        |

Heritage Butter (product 28) sold on 779 order lines, to 78 different customers.

## Case file: how much was never invoiced?

This series follows one case through Northwind Company. In 2024 its ordered sales grew while its invoiced sales fell; the orders marked shipped with no ship date turned out to be one courier's, and they started in March and stopped at the end of August. The question still open is **how much money**. Every shipped order should have an invoice in `"Invoices"`, so the report is: the orders marked shipped that have no invoice, and what they are worth.

It is this lesson's shape again. An order's value comes from its **lines**; the invoice is at most one row per **order**. So each order's value goes first, in brackets, one row per order; then the orders `LEFT JOIN` the invoices, and `IS NULL` keeps the ones with no match, as in the JOINs lesson:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the brackets, in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/shipped-orders-without-invoice.sql {4-8}#good
SELECT count(*) AS "Orders",
       ROUND(SUM(v."Order value"), 2) AS "Value"
FROM "Orders" o
JOIN (SELECT "OrderID",
             SUM("UnitPrice" * "Quantity"
               * (1 - "Discount")) AS "Order value"
      FROM "Order Details"
      GROUP BY "OrderID") AS v
  ON v."OrderID" = o."OrderID"
LEFT JOIN "Invoices" i
  ON i."OrderID" = o."OrderID"
WHERE o."Status" = 'Shipped'
  AND i."InvoiceID" IS NULL;
```

{/* The same highlight as the video: the row, in grey — the finding, no verdict. */}

Before the `WHERE`, one row is one order, so `count(*)` counts orders. With `"Order Details"` joined in directly instead of in brackets, the value would come out the same but `count(*)` would say 460: lines, not orders.

In Northwind Company, **188 orders are marked shipped and have no invoice, worth 275698.44.** That is how much money the question is about. It does not say whether those orders were delivered: the data has not answered that yet, and this series comes back to it.

## Four rules for joins

1. **Count the rows before and after every join.**
2. **An `INNER JOIN` after a `LEFT JOIN` drops what the `LEFT JOIN` kept.**
3. **Narrow or aggregate before the join**, in brackets, not after it.
4. **Sum a column only at its own grain.** `SUM(DISTINCT …)` removes repeated values, not repeated orders.
