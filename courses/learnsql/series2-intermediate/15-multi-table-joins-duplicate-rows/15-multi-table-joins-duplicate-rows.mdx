## Two things a join does without a word

In the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/select-sum-join-group-by-order-by-limit-project" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">last lesson of Series 1</a>, a join multiplied the rows of a report, and `count(DISTINCT …)` put the count right. This lesson is about the two cases that lesson left out, because they are harder to see:

1. **A join that drops rows** that a `LEFT JOIN` was supposed to keep.
2. **A join that multiplies money**: a total that is too big, with nothing in the result to show it.

Neither one produces an error. Both are about the same question: **one row of this result is one what?** At the end, the same tool answers the question the dates lesson left open: whose orders have no ship date, and how much money is on them.

Every query below runs on the Northwind database on PostgreSQL. Write each one in CloudBeaver and compare the result with the table under it. If that is not set up yet, the [Learn Data](/docs/learn-data) guide gets it running.

## Every product, and its June units

The first report: **every product, with the units it sold in June 2024**, including the products that sold nothing. "Every product" means a `LEFT JOIN` from `"Products"`, as in the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/joins" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">JOINs lesson</a>, and June is a half-open range, as in the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/dates-and-times" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">dates lesson</a>. The June test goes in the `ON`:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the two joins, in grey. */}

```sql file=./scripts/june-units-left-then-inner.sql {4,6}
SELECT p."ProductName",
       sum(d."Quantity") AS "June units"
FROM "Products" p
LEFT JOIN "Order Details" d
  ON d."ProductID" = p."ProductID"
JOIN "Orders" o
  ON o."OrderID" = d."OrderID"
 AND o."OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-06-01'
 AND o."OrderDate" <  DATE '2024-07-01'
GROUP BY p."ProductName"
ORDER BY p."ProductName";
```

| ProductName                 | June units |
| --------------------------- | ---------- |
| Aniseed Syrup               | 3          |
| Boston Crab Meat            | 8          |
| Chai                        | 12         |
| Chang                       | 10         |
| Chef Antons Cajun Seasoning | 2          |
| Scottish Longbreads         | 5          |

**Count the rows before and after.** Twenty products went in; six rows came out. The query still says `LEFT JOIN`, and fourteen products are missing.

The `LEFT JOIN` to `"Order Details"` kept every product. Then the plain `JOIN` to `"Orders"` kept only the rows that matched a June order, and removed every other row, including the ones the `LEFT JOIN` had kept. **An inner join after a left join drops what the left join kept.** It is the ON-versus-WHERE problem from the JOINs lesson, one table further along.

## LEFT all the way: it looks repaired

The obvious fix is to make the second join a `LEFT JOIN` too:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the second LEFT JOIN, in grey. */}

```sql file=./scripts/june-units-left-all-the-way.sql {6}
SELECT p."ProductName",
       sum(d."Quantity") AS "June units"
FROM "Products" p
LEFT JOIN "Order Details" d
  ON d."ProductID" = p."ProductID"
LEFT JOIN "Orders" o
  ON o."OrderID" = d."OrderID"
 AND o."OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-06-01'
 AND o."OrderDate" <  DATE '2024-07-01'
GROUP BY p."ProductName"
ORDER BY p."ProductName";
```

## Narrow first, then join

The fix is to answer the June question first, in brackets, and then join the products to the answer. The brackets add up June's lines per product — a table in `FROM`, from the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/subqueries" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">subqueries lesson</a> — and the products are `LEFT JOIN`ed to that table:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the June range, in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/june-units-narrowed-first.sql {10-11}#good
SELECT p."ProductName",
       j."June units"
FROM "Products" p
LEFT JOIN (SELECT d."ProductID",
                  sum(d."Quantity")
                    AS "June units"
           FROM "Order Details" d
           JOIN "Orders" o
             ON o."OrderID" = d."OrderID"
           WHERE o."OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-06-01'
             AND o."OrderDate" <  DATE '2024-07-01'
           GROUP BY d."ProductID") AS j
  ON j."ProductID" = p."ProductID"
ORDER BY p."ProductName";
```

## A join that multiplies money

The manager wants **sales and freight per customer** on one report. It is the customer report from the end of Series 1 with one more `SUM`:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the freight line, in grey. */}

```sql file=./scripts/sales-and-freight-per-customer.sql {4}

       ROUND(SUM(d."UnitPrice" * d."Quantity"
         * (1 - d."Discount")), 2) AS "Total sales",

JOIN "Orders" o
  ON o."CustomerID" = c."CustomerID"
JOIN "Order Details" d
```

### Why the freight grew

Look at Folk och fä HB's three orders and how many lines each one has:

```sql file=./scripts/folk-och-fa-orders-and-lines.sql
SELECT o."OrderID", o."Freight",
       count(*) AS "Lines"
FROM "Orders" o
JOIN "Order Details" d
  ON d."OrderID" = o."OrderID"
WHERE o."CustomerID" = 'FOLKO'
GROUP BY o."OrderID", o."Freight"
ORDER BY o."OrderID";
```

| OrderID | Freight | Lines |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| 19      | 87.33   | 3     |
| 44      | 82.08   | 2     |
| 69      | 77.83   | 3     |

`"Freight"` is stored once per order. After joining `"Order Details"`, one row is one **order line**, and each line carries a copy of its order's freight. `SUM` adds every copy: 87.33 × 3 + 82.08 × 2 + 77.83 × 3 = 659.64. An order with more lines gets its freight counted more times.

Across the whole company:

```sql file=./scripts/company-freight-two-ways.sql
SELECT (SELECT SUM("Freight")
        FROM "Orders") AS "Orders alone",
       (SELECT SUM(o."Freight")
        FROM "Orders" o
        JOIN "Order Details" d
          ON d."OrderID" = o."OrderID")
         AS "After the join";
```

| Orders alone | After the join |
| ------------ | -------------- |
| 3988.52      | 9871.56        |

The joined total is nearly two and a half times the real freight bill, and nothing reported a problem. The sales column in the first report was correct all along: sales are calculated from the lines, and one row is one line.

## SUM(DISTINCT) is luck

Series 1 fixed a multiplied count with `DISTINCT`, so it is tempting to write `SUM(DISTINCT o."Freight")`:

```sql file=./scripts/sum-distinct-freight.sql
SELECT SUM(DISTINCT o."Freight")
         AS "SUM(DISTINCT)"
FROM "Orders" o
JOIN "Order Details" d
  ON d."OrderID" = o."OrderID";
```

| SUM(DISTINCT) |
| ------------- |
| 3988.52       |

It returns exactly 3988.52, but only by luck. `SUM(DISTINCT …)` adds each distinct **value** once. It does not know about orders. It gives the right answer here because no two of the 79 orders have the same freight. Here are three orders written into the query, two of them with the same freight:

{/* The same highlight as the video: SUM(DISTINCT) in red. */}

```sql file=./scripts/sum-distinct-shared-freight.sql {1}#err
SELECT SUM(DISTINCT "Freight") AS "SUM(DISTINCT)",
       SUM("Freight") AS "Real total"
FROM (VALUES (1, 10.00),
             (2, 10.00),
             (3, 25.50)) AS t("OrderID", "Freight");
```

| SUM(DISTINCT) | Real total |
| ------------- | ---------- |
| 35.5          | 45.5       |

`SUM(DISTINCT)` keeps one of the two 10.00 values and loses a whole order. On real data, the first time two orders share a freight value, the report is wrong again without any sign.

## Aggregate first, then join

The correct version adds up each measure where one row is one of its own, and joins the results afterwards: sales per customer from the order lines, freight per customer from `"Orders"` alone, each in its own brackets.

{/* The same highlight as the video: the freight brackets, in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/sales-and-freight-aggregated-first.sql {11-14}#good
SELECT c."CompanyName", s."Total sales", f."Freight"
FROM "Customers" c
JOIN (SELECT o."CustomerID",
             ROUND(SUM(d."UnitPrice" * d."Quantity"
               * (1 - d."Discount")), 2) AS "Total sales"
      FROM "Orders" o
      JOIN "Order Details" d
        ON d."OrderID" = o."OrderID"
      GROUP BY o."CustomerID") AS s
  ON s."CustomerID" = c."CustomerID"
JOIN (SELECT "CustomerID",
             SUM("Freight") AS "Freight"
      FROM "Orders"
      GROUP BY "CustomerID") AS f
  ON f."CustomerID" = c."CustomerID"
ORDER BY f."Freight" DESC
LIMIT 5;
```

## One row is one what?

Before adding up a column after a join, ask the question from the end of Series 1: **one row of this result is one what?** After joining the order lines, one row is one order line. Freight belongs to one order, so it can only be summed where one row is one order. Sales belong to one line, so they can be summed over the lines. This is called the **grain** of a table or a result, and the rule is:

**Sum each measure at its own grain, then join the results.**

### A bridge table

`"Order Details"` is a **bridge** table: it connects orders and products, which have a many-to-many relationship. Through a bridge, `count(*)` counts lines. Count something else only when you say so:

```sql file=./scripts/chai-lines-and-customers.sql
SELECT count(*) AS "Lines",
       count(DISTINCT o."CustomerID")
         AS "Customers"
FROM "Order Details" d
JOIN "Orders" o
  ON o."OrderID" = d."OrderID"
WHERE d."ProductID" = 1;
```

| Lines | Customers |
| ----- | --------- |
| 10    | 8         |

Chai sold on 10 order lines to 8 different customers.

## Case file: whose orders, and how much money?

This series follows one case through Northwind. In the subqueries lesson, 19169 of the 58153.31 it sold turned out to be on orders with no ship date, and the dates lesson showed those orders going back to the first quarter of the data. What was left to find is where that money sits: which rep took the orders, and which courier was meant to deliver them.

That report mixes three grains. An order's value comes from its **lines**. Its freight is stored once on the **order**. The rep and the courier are one row each in `"Employees"` and `"Shippers"`. So the value goes first, in brackets, one row per order, exactly like the freight per customer above. Then everything joins to one row per order:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the brackets, in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/no-ship-date-by-rep-and-courier.sql {7-11}#good
SELECT e."FirstName" AS "Rep",
       s."CompanyName" AS "Courier",
       count(*) AS "Orders",
       ROUND(SUM(v."Order value"), 2) AS "Sales",
       SUM(o."Freight") AS "Freight"
FROM "Orders" o
JOIN (SELECT "OrderID",
             SUM("UnitPrice" * "Quantity"
               * (1 - "Discount")) AS "Order value"
      FROM "Order Details"
      GROUP BY "OrderID") AS v
  ON v."OrderID" = o."OrderID"
JOIN "Employees" e
  ON e."EmployeeID" = o."EmployeeID"
JOIN "Shippers" s
  ON s."ShipperID" = o."ShipVia"
WHERE o."ShippedDate" IS NULL
GROUP BY e."FirstName", s."CompanyName"
ORDER BY "Sales" DESC;
```

Before the `GROUP BY`, one row is one order, so `count(*)` counts orders and `SUM(o."Freight")` adds each freight once. The three rows hold all 27 orders with no ship date and all 19169 of the money. With `"Order Details"` joined in directly instead of in brackets, the first row's freight would read 3185.64.

In Northwind, the first row is Nancy's: 24 of the 27 are her orders sent with Speedy Express, and they carry 18848.3 of the 19169. That says where the money sits. It does not say why, and a number is not a verdict: this series comes back to it.

## Four rules for joins

1. **Count the rows before and after every join.**
2. **An `INNER JOIN` after a `LEFT JOIN` drops what the `LEFT JOIN` kept.**
3. **Narrow or aggregate before the join**, in brackets, not after it.
4. **Sum a column only at its own grain.** `SUM(DISTINCT …)` is right only by luck.
