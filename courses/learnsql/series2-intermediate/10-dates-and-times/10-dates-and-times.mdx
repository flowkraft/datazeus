## Orders per month

The manager wants a chart: **orders per month**, one bar for every month the business has been trading.

It sounds like something Series 1 already covered. The <a href="/academy/learn-sql/group-by" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">GROUP BY lesson</a> used `EXTRACT` to pull the month out of a date, and grouping by that gives one row per month. This lesson starts there, because that query returns a chart that looks reasonable and is wrong. The rest of the lesson is about getting the edges of dates right: months and weeks, partial periods, ranges, "recent", the difference between two dates, and the months that have no rows at all. At the end, the same tools take the case from the last two lessons one step further: the orders marked shipped with no ship date, and since when.

Every query below runs on Northwind Company, the dataset installed in the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/subqueries" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">first lesson of this series</a>: five years of orders, from 1 January 2020 to 31 December 2024. Each query assumes you have run `SET search_path TO northwind_co_s;` in the CloudBeaver editor first. Run each one and compare the result with the table under it.

## EXTRACT throws the year away

```sql file=./scripts/orders-per-month-extract.sql {1,5}
         AS "Month",
       count(*) AS "Orders"
FROM "Orders"
GROUP BY EXTRACT(MONTH FROM "OrderDate")
ORDER BY "Month";
```

Drawn as a chart, this looks right: November and December are the busiest months and August the quietest, and that part is true. The growth from one June to the next disappears with them.

## date_trunc keeps the year

`date_trunc('month', …)` cuts a date back to the first day of its month and keeps the year. 5 June 2024 becomes 1 June 2024, and 5 June 2023 becomes 1 June 2023, so the Junes stay apart.

{/* The same highlight as the video: the date_trunc lines, in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/orders-per-month.sql {1,5}#good
SELECT date_trunc('month', "OrderDate")
         AS "Month",
       count(*) AS "Orders"
FROM "Orders"
GROUP BY date_trunc('month', "OrderDate")
ORDER BY "Month";
```

| Month                   | Orders |
| ----------------------- | ------ |
| 2020-01-01 00:00:00.000 | 138    |
| 2020-02-01 00:00:00.000 | 128    |
| 2020-03-01 00:00:00.000 | 126    |
| …                       | …      |
| 2024-09-01 00:00:00.000 | 168    |
| 2024-10-01 00:00:00.000 | 205    |
| 2024-11-01 00:00:00.000 | 277    |
| 2024-12-01 00:00:00.000 | 254    |

60 rows, one for each real month, from 90 orders (August 2020) to 277 (November 2024). The count did not change; only the label did, and now the company's growth is visible.

On PostgreSQL `date_trunc` returns a timestamp, so CloudBeaver shows the midnight too. DuckDB returns a date for the same query; the months are the same.

## Weeks, and the edges of the data

`date_trunc` also accepts `'quarter'`, `'year'` and `'week'`. A week starts on a Monday. This time, also ask for the first and last order in each week:

{/* The same highlight as the video: date_trunc and the first/last order, in grey. */}

```sql file=./scripts/orders-per-week.sql {1,4-5}
SELECT date_trunc('week', "OrderDate")
         AS "Week",
       count(*) AS "Orders",
       min("OrderDate") AS "First order",
       max("OrderDate") AS "Last order"
FROM "Orders"
GROUP BY date_trunc('week', "OrderDate")
ORDER BY "Week";
```

262 rows. The last week looks like a crash, 60 orders and then 20, but its last order is on 31 December, the last day of the data: **that week has two days in it**, not seven. The first row is labelled 2019-12-30, a date in 2019, before the data starts, because the week began on that Monday; the data starts on Wednesday 1 January 2020, so the first week has five days. **Both are partial.** One looks like a crash and could just as easily look like a record, so a partial period cannot be recognised by its count. The first and last dates show it, and the report should say it.

In this data every month, quarter and year is whole, because the data starts on 1 January and ends on 31 December. On a live database the current month is always partial, and it is the one people read first.

## Half-open ranges

The fix is a habit rather than a better end date: **on or after the first day, and strictly before the first day of the next period.**

{/* The same highlight as the video: the two edges, in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/shipped-in-november-half-open.sql {4-5}#good
SELECT count(*) AS "Shipments",
       max("ShippedDate") AS "Last shipped"
FROM "Orders"
WHERE "ShippedDate" >= DATE '2024-11-01'
  AND "ShippedDate" <  DATE '2024-12-01';
```

<div className="[&_tbody_tr]:bg-[rgb(var(--hl-good)/var(--hl-row))] [&_tbody_tr_td]:font-bold [&_tbody_tr_td]:text-[rgb(var(--hl-good-ink))]">

| Shipments | Last shipped            |
| --------- | ----------------------- |
| 263       | 2024-11-30 00:00:00.000 |

</div>

263 for November, the last one on 30 November, and the 14 shipments of 1 December belong to December only. When every month is written this way, the end of one month is the start of the next, and no day can fall into two of them.

### Ending on the last day is no way out

The other repair people reach for is `BETWEEN DATE '2024-11-01' AND DATE '2024-11-30'`. On `"ShippedDate"` it happens to give 263, because every ship date in this table is at midnight. `"CreatedAt"`, the moment each order was keyed in, is not: it carries the time of day, between 08:00 and 17:59. Count November's orders by `"CreatedAt"` both ways, with the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/case-expressions" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">CASE lesson</a>'s conditional count:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the BETWEEN count in red, the half-open count in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/keyed-in-november.sql {1-2,4-5}
SELECT SUM(CASE WHEN "CreatedAt" BETWEEN DATE '2024-11-01'
                                     AND DATE '2024-11-30'
           THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS "Ends on the 30th",
       SUM(CASE WHEN "CreatedAt" >= DATE '2024-11-01'
                 AND "CreatedAt" <  DATE '2024-12-01'
           THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS "Half-open"
FROM "Orders";
```

| Ends on the 30th | Half-open |
| ---------------- | --------- |
| 271              | 278       |

Against a timestamp, `DATE '2024-11-30'` means midnight at the **start** of 30 November, so the 7 orders keyed in during that day are later than the end of the range and silently drop out. This is the half-open range from the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/where-filtering" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">WHERE lesson</a>, and here it is not a hypothetical: the same table has a column where `BETWEEN` happens to work and a column where it loses a day. The half-open range does not depend on which one you are querying.

## "Recent" on a snapshot

The last 30 days, counted from today:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the CURRENT_DATE lines, in red. */}

```sql file=./scripts/last-30-days.sql {3-4}#err
SELECT count(*) AS "Orders"
FROM "Orders"
WHERE "OrderDate" >= CURRENT_DATE
                     - INTERVAL '30 days';
```

| Orders |
| ------ |
| 0      |

The query is correct. `CURRENT_DATE` is today, and this data stops on 31 December 2024, so no order is less than 30 days old. On a live database, `CURRENT_DATE` is exactly what "recent" should mean. On a snapshot like this one, anchor "recent" on the data instead: the last order date, calculated in brackets with a <a href="/academy/learn-sql/subqueries" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">subquery</a>. Ask for the first and last order as well, to see the edges:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the anchor, in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/last-30-days-of-data.sql {6}#good
SELECT count(*) AS "Orders",
       min("OrderDate") AS "From",
       max("OrderDate") AS "To"
FROM "Orders"
WHERE "OrderDate" >=
      (SELECT max("OrderDate") FROM "Orders")
      - INTERVAL '30 days';
```

| Orders | From                    | To                      |
| ------ | ----------------------- | ----------------------- |
| 254    | 2024-12-01 00:00:00.000 | 2024-12-31 00:00:00.000 |

254 orders, from 1 to 31 December. A timestamp minus an `INTERVAL` is still a timestamp, so `max("OrderDate") - INTERVAL '30 days'` is midnight on 1 December 2024, and `>=` keeps that whole day.

## Subtracting dates

Which orders took more than a week to ship? The obvious query subtracts one date from the other:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the subtraction, in red. */}

```sql file=./scripts/slow-orders-interval-error.sql {3}#err
SELECT count(*) AS "Slow orders"
FROM "Orders"
WHERE "ShippedDate" - "OrderDate" > 7;
```

PostgreSQL refuses it:

```
SQL Error [42883]: ERROR: operator does not exist: interval > integer
  Hint: No operator matches the given name and argument types. You might need to add explicit type casts.
  Position: 82
```

`"OrderDate"` and `"ShippedDate"` are timestamps, and **a timestamp minus a timestamp is an `INTERVAL`**: a length of time such as `4 days`, not the number 4. An interval cannot be compared with a plain number. DuckDB refuses the query too.

**A date minus a date is a whole number of days.** Cast both sides to `DATE` first:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the two casts, in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/slow-orders.sql {3-4}#good
SELECT count(*) AS "Slow orders"
FROM "Orders"
WHERE CAST("ShippedDate" AS DATE)
      - CAST("OrderDate" AS DATE) > 7;
```

| Slow orders |
| ----------- |
| 193         |

This is why the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/null-and-three-valued-logic" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">NULL lesson</a> cast both sides when it measured days to ship. `"ShippedDate" - "OrderDate" > INTERVAL '7 days'` also works and returns the same 193; the cast is the version that gives you a number you can round, average and show.

The days for the first five orders:

```sql file=./scripts/days-to-ship.sql
SELECT "OrderID", "OrderDate", "ShippedDate",
       CAST("ShippedDate" AS DATE)
       - CAST("OrderDate" AS DATE) AS "Days"
FROM "Orders"
ORDER BY "OrderID"
LIMIT 5;
```

| OrderID | OrderDate               | ShippedDate             | Days |
| ------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------- | ---- |
| 1       | 2020-01-01 00:00:00.000 | 2020-01-05 00:00:00.000 | 4    |
| 2       | 2020-01-01 00:00:00.000 | 2020-01-04 00:00:00.000 | 3    |
| 3       | 2020-01-01 00:00:00.000 | 2020-01-05 00:00:00.000 | 4    |
| 4       | 2020-01-01 00:00:00.000 | 2020-01-04 00:00:00.000 | 3    |
| 5       | 2020-01-01 00:00:00.000 | 2020-01-07 00:00:00.000 | 6    |

An order with no ship date has no days: its `"Days"` is NULL, not 0. There are 468 such orders, and `AVG` skips all of them:

```sql file=./scripts/average-days-to-ship.sql
SELECT ROUND(AVG(CAST("ShippedDate" AS DATE)
         - CAST("OrderDate" AS DATE)), 1)
         AS "Average days",
       max(CAST("ShippedDate" AS DATE)
         - CAST("OrderDate" AS DATE))
         AS "Longest"
FROM "Orders";
```

| Average days | Longest |
| ------------ | ------- |
| 4.8          | 24      |

A normal shipment here takes 2 to 7 days; the 193 slow ones took 15 to 24. PostgreSQL can also average the interval itself (`4 days 19:54:48…`), and has an `AGE()` function that describes the gap between two timestamps in years, months and days. Both are PostgreSQL's own. The DuckDB the koans run on refuses to average an interval, so this course casts to `DATE` and works in days.

### Comparing two timestamps needs no arithmetic

`"RequiredDate"` is when the customer needs the order. Which orders shipped in December 2024 after that?

{/* The same highlight as the video: the comparison, in grey. */}

```sql file=./scripts/late-orders.sql {4}
SELECT "OrderID",
       "RequiredDate", "ShippedDate"
FROM "Orders"
WHERE "ShippedDate" > "RequiredDate"
  AND "ShippedDate" >= DATE '2024-12-01'
  AND "ShippedDate" <  DATE '2025-01-01'
ORDER BY "ShippedDate", "OrderID";
```

| OrderID | RequiredDate            | ShippedDate             |
| ------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------- |
| 9720    | 2024-12-12 00:00:00.000 | 2024-12-15 00:00:00.000 |
| 9696    | 2024-12-10 00:00:00.000 | 2024-12-20 00:00:00.000 |
| 9725    | 2024-12-12 00:00:00.000 | 2024-12-20 00:00:00.000 |

Three orders. Order 9696 was needed on 10 December and shipped ten days later. Orders 9696 and 9725 both shipped on the 20th, which is why the `ORDER BY` names `"OrderID"` too: without it, the two could come back in either order.

## The months that have no rows

For the whole company there is hardly a day without an order, so a company-wide chart never shows this problem. One customer's chart does. Here is Bramble Supermarket's month by month in 2024:

{/* The same highlight as the video: date_trunc('month', …), in grey. */}

```sql file=./scripts/orders-per-month-one-customer.sql {1,7}
SELECT date_trunc('month', "OrderDate") AS "Month",
       count(*) AS "Orders"
FROM "Orders"
WHERE "CustomerID" = 'BRAM3'
  AND "OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-01-01'
  AND "OrderDate" <  DATE '2025-01-01'
GROUP BY date_trunc('month', "OrderDate")
ORDER BY "Month";
```

| Month                   | Orders |
| ----------------------- | ------ |
| 2024-01-01 00:00:00.000 | 2      |
| 2024-05-01 00:00:00.000 | 1      |
| 2024-10-01 00:00:00.000 | 1      |
| 2024-11-01 00:00:00.000 | 2      |

Four rows. Nothing happened in February, so there is no row for it, and a chart drawn from this result joins January to May with a line. `GROUP BY` can only report months that have rows. **The year has 8 months with no orders, and the result does not show any of them.**

### A date spine

The fix is to generate every month first, then attach the orders to the months. `generate_series` produces one timestamp per month from January to December, and a `LEFT JOIN` from the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/joins" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">JOINs lesson</a> keeps every month, including the months with no matching order. The customer's condition goes in the `ON`: in a `WHERE` it would throw away the empty months again, the trap that lesson showed.

{/* The same highlight as the video: generate_series, in green; the customer's condition in grey. */}

```sql file=./scripts/customer-month-spine.sql {3-6}#good
SELECT s.month AS "Month",
       count(o."OrderID") AS "Orders"
FROM generate_series(
       TIMESTAMP '2024-01-01',
       TIMESTAMP '2024-12-01',
       INTERVAL '1 month') AS s(month)
LEFT JOIN "Orders" o
  ON date_trunc('month', o."OrderDate") = s.month
 AND o."CustomerID" = 'BRAM3'
GROUP BY s.month
ORDER BY s.month;
```

A table of every date, stored permanently, is called a calendar table; `generate_series` builds the same thing when the query runs. It exists in PostgreSQL and DuckDB (with `INTERVAL '1 day'` it builds days just as well); other databases spell it differently.

### count(*) counts the rows

For Bramble's total for the year, it is tempting to use `count(*)` over the spine. Run it next to `count(o."OrderID")`:

{/* The same highlight as the video: the two counts, count(*) in red and count(OrderID) in green. */}

```sql file=./scripts/customer-spine-count-star.sql {1-2}
SELECT count(*) AS "count(*)",
       count(o."OrderID") AS "count(OrderID)"
FROM generate_series(
       TIMESTAMP '2024-01-01',
       TIMESTAMP '2024-12-01',
       INTERVAL '1 month') AS s(month)
LEFT JOIN "Orders" o
  ON date_trunc('month', o."OrderDate") = s.month
 AND o."CustomerID" = 'BRAM3';
```

| count(*) | count(OrderID) |
| -------- | -------------- |
| 14       | 6              |

`count(*)` counts rows: one row for each of the 6 orders, and one row for each of the 8 empty months, 14 in all. That is neither the orders nor the months, which is exactly why it is easy to miss. On an empty month `o."OrderID"` is NULL, and <a href="/academy/learn-sql/aggregate-functions" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">count of a column skips NULLs</a>, so `count(o."OrderID")` returns the real 6.

## The case: since when?

The <a href="/academy/learn-sql/subqueries" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">subqueries lesson</a> found that in Northwind Company, what was invoiced in 2024 falls well short of what was ordered, and the <a href="/academy/learn-sql/case-expressions" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="font-medium underline underline-offset-4">CASE lesson</a> found where to look: the orders marked shipped with no ship date all go through one courier. The next question is **since when?** Nothing below is new SQL: it is this lesson's `date_trunc('week', …)` with the CASE lesson's conditional count.

{/* The same highlight as the video: date_trunc and the conditional count, in grey. */}

```sql file=./scripts/no-ship-date-per-week.sql {1,3-5}
SELECT date_trunc('week', "OrderDate") AS "Week",
       count(*) AS "Orders",
       SUM(CASE WHEN "Status" = 'Shipped'
                 AND "ShippedDate" IS NULL
           THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS "No ship date"
FROM "Orders"
WHERE "OrderDate" >= DATE '2024-01-01'
  AND "OrderDate" <  DATE '2025-01-01'
GROUP BY date_trunc('week', "OrderDate")
ORDER BY "Week";
```

Every week of 2024 has orders, so this query returns all 53 weeks, and a week with none of these orders shows 0 rather than going missing. (Filtering the orders in the `WHERE` and counting rows would have handed back only the weeks that have some, which is the problem of the section above.) | 2024-03-11 00:00:00.000 | 53     | 8            |
| …                       | …      | …            |
| 2024-08-19 00:00:00.000 | 35     | 7            |

Every week from January to the end of February shows 0. In the week of 4 March there are 8, and **every week after it has between 3 and 12**, up to the week of 26 August. From the week of 2 September it is 0 again, apart from a single order in the week of 28 October. It did not creep in slowly or build up like a busy season: **it started in one week and stopped in another**, and 26 weeks sit between the two.

So, in Northwind Company: the orders marked shipped with no ship date come from the six months between early March and the end of August 2024, a few every week. A missing ship date is not proof that nothing was delivered, and SQL alone will not decide that; people who can check with the courier will. What this query cannot say yet is how much money is on those orders. That needs more than one table.

## Four habits for dates

1. **Group by `date_trunc`, not `EXTRACT`**, so the year stays in the group, and ask for the first and last date so a partial period shows itself.
2. **Write ranges half-open:** `>=` the start and `<` the next start. `BETWEEN` counts a boundary twice or misses the rest of the last day.
3. **Anchor "recent" on the data** when the data is not live.
4. **Cast to `DATE` before you subtract**, and over a date spine **count the orders, not the rows.**
