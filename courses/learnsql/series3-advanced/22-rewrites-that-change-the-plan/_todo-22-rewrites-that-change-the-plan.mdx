    ── THE FOUR PATTERNS, WRONG VERSION FIRST ──────────────────────────────

    1. ASKING WHETHER A ROW EXISTS — three ways, and they are not equivalent:
         COUNT(*) > 0   counts every matching row to answer yes or no
         EXISTS         stops at the first row it finds. This is the one you want.
         JOIN           finds every match, so it can MULTIPLY your rows — Series 2 · 06's
                        fan-out arriving as a performance problem instead of a wrong total

       AND THE CORRECTNESS BOMB, which is why this pattern leads:
       NOT IN (subquery) RETURNS NO ROWS AT ALL IF THE SUBQUERY PRODUCES A SINGLE NULL.
       Not fewer rows — none. It is Series 1 · 45's three-valued logic detonating inside a
       construct that looks nothing like a NULL comparison, it is silent, and it is common.
       NOT EXISTS does not behave this way. Teach the habit: NOT EXISTS, always.

    2. DISTINCT AS A PLASTER — ask the diagnostic question: did you add DISTINCT because
       you wanted unique values, or because you were getting duplicates? The second is a
       fan-out you have hidden rather than fixed, and DISTINCT pays for the concealment
       with a sort or hash over the whole result set. Fix the grain — Series 2 · 06 owns
       how — and the DISTINCT disappears along with the cost.
       UNION against UNION ALL is the same lesson in a second costume: UNION deduplicates,
       and that is a sort you are buying. If the two sides cannot overlap, say ALL.

    3. OR ACROSS COLUMNS — `WHERE city = 'London' OR country = 'UK'` often cannot use
       either index, because no single index satisfies both branches. Two indexed queries
       combined with UNION ALL frequently can. Show the plan before and after, and mark
       this as the most ENGINE-DEPENDENT item in the episode: some optimizers do it for
       you, and the reader should check rather than assume.

    4. DEEP PAGINATION — the one every reader will use.
       OFFSET 100000 LIMIT 20 must PRODUCE AND DISCARD one hundred thousand rows to reach
       page 5001. Cost grows with the page number, so the LAST page of a report is the
       slowest — backwards from what everyone expects, and why "the report is slow" is
       sometimes only true for one person.
       THE KEYSET (seek) ALTERNATIVE: remember the last row's sort key and ask for what
       comes after it —
           WHERE (order_date, order_id) < (:last_date, :last_id)
           ORDER BY order_date DESC, order_id DESC
           LIMIT 20
       Constant cost per page.
       STATE THE TRADE HONESTLY: you can no longer jump to page 900, and the ORDER BY MUST
       END IN SOMETHING UNIQUE. Without a unique tiebreaker, paging over ties is
       non-deterministic — a row can appear on two pages or on none. That is a correctness
       bug that only shows up in production, and it deserves as much airtime as the speed.

    ── ONE PARAGRAPH ON CTEs, BECAUSE SOMEBODY WILL ASK ────────────────────
