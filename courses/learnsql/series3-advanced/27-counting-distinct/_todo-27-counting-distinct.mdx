    ── WHY THIS EPISODE, SPECIFICALLY ──────────────────────────────────────
    GOAL: know why distinct counting behaves unlike every other aggregate, and have four
    answers for it — ask whether it must be exact, pre-aggregate to a distinct grain,
    reshape the query, or approximate.
    WHY IT SITS AFTER INDEXES: half the value is the sentence "you have indexed it and it
    is still slow, and here is why an index was never going to fix this."
    seed: bulk — on 79 rows there is nothing to feel.

    ── THE IDEA THE EPISODE EXISTS FOR, IN ONE BREATH ──────────────────────
        SUM, COUNT, MIN and MAX DECOMPOSE. COUNT(DISTINCT) DOES NOT.

    A sum of daily sums is the monthly sum. A count of daily counts is the monthly count.
    The distinct customers in January PLUS the distinct customers in February IS NOT the
    distinct customers over both months — anyone who bought in both has been counted
    twice.

    That single fact explains, all at once: why you cannot pre-aggregate it into a summary
    table, why a warehouse rollup gets it wrong, why it does not split cheaply across
    workers or partitions, and why the query is slow.

    ── THEN THE MECHANISM, BRIEFLY ─────────────────────────────────────────
    To count distinct values the engine must HOLD THEM ALL — a hash set or a sort — so
    cost and memory scale with the number of DISTINCT values, not with the size of the
    answer. Past the memory grant it spills to disk and falls off a cliff. Several
    COUNT(DISTINCT) expressions in one SELECT means several such sets at once, and some
    engines handle that far worse than one.

    ── THE FOUR TECHNIQUES, PORTABLE FIRST ─────────────────────────────────
      ASK WHETHER IT MUST BE EXACT       a business question, not a technical one, and the
                                        reader should be taught to ask it out loud
      PRE-AGGREGATE TO A DISTINCT GRAIN  one row per (day, customer), or a first-seen date
                                        per customer, and count over something far
                                        smaller. The honest exact answer.
      GROUP BY THEN COUNT                SELECT COUNT(*) FROM (SELECT DISTINCT ...)
                                        sometimes plans better. NOT a rule — a thing to
                                        measure, with EXPLAIN from 20.
      APPROXIMATE IT                     HyperLogLog: a fixed-size sketch, typically one or
                                        two per cent error, and the size does not grow
                                        with the data.

    ── THE DEEP POINT ABOUT APPROXIMATION ──────────────────────────────────
    SKETCHES MERGE. Two HLL sketches combine into the sketch of the union, so an
    approximate distinct count IS decomposable — store one sketch per day and answer any
    date range from them. Approximation does not merely make the query faster: it gives
    back the property the exact aggregate lacks, which is the entire reason warehouses
    use it. That is what makes this an episode rather than a footnote.

    WHAT EACH ENGINE OFFERS, named and left there — the engine tracks own the detail:
    Oracle and SQL Server both ship APPROX_COUNT_DISTINCT; PostgreSQL has no built-in and
    uses the postgresql-hll extension; MySQL has nothing; ClickHouse's uniq family and
    DuckDB's approx_count_distinct are why analytical engines answer this instantly.

    ONE LINE ON WINDOWS: COUNT(DISTINCT ...) OVER (...) is unsupported on several engines,
    which surprises people who reach for it after Series 2 · 35.
