       it wants RAM, and on a small box the ARC competes with the database you are running;
       A POOL CANNOT BE SHRUNK, and vdevs largely cannot be removed — a layout mistake is
         permanent in a way ext4 never is;
       the licence keeps it out of the mainline kernel, so it is a module your distribution
         may or may not make easy, and a kernel upgrade can break it;
       and if you have hardware RAID underneath, ZFS cannot see the disks and its whole
         self-healing argument evaporates — which is the commonest way people get none of
         the benefit and all of the complexity.
     SAY IT PLAINLY: on a rented VM with one virtual disk, ext4 or XFS is the right answer and
     this series is background reading.
     ADDED as its own episode 2026-09-07. It had been a paragraph inside Series 1 · 19, beside
     ext4 and XFS, which was the wrong neighbour: btrfs is a COPY-ON-WRITE filesystem, so the
     comparison a reader needs is with ZFS. And it is placed immediately before 22 on purpose,
     because "use btrfs instead" is one of the real answers to "do you need ZFS?" and that
     episode cannot weigh an option this course never taught.
     COVER:
       THE ONE ADVANTAGE THAT DECIDES MOST DEBIAN CASES: IT IS IN THE MAINLINE KERNEL. No
                       DKMS, no module rebuilt against every kernel upgrade, no licence
                       question — `apt install btrfs-progs` and the filesystem is already
                       there. Series 2 · 02 named the DKMS build as where Debian readers get
                       stuck; this is the option that does not have that problem at all.
       SUBVOLUMES      not quite ZFS datasets — cheaper, and mountable as directories, which
                       is why distributions use them for root and /home layouts.
       SNAPSHOTS       near-instant, and WRITABLE by default, which is the opposite of ZFS's
                       default and surprises people moving between them.
       CHECKSUMS AND SCRUB   the same argument as 2 · 10 — it detects corruption, and with
                       redundancy it repairs it. This is the reason to consider it at all.
       COMPRESSION     zstd as a mount option, per-file, and the same "fewer bytes leave the
                       disk" argument that makes it a performance win rather than a cost.
       send/receive    exists, and does the job 2 · 15 uses ZFS's for.
     THE CAUTION, STATED CAREFULLY BECAUSE IT IS THE MOST-QUOTED AND LEAST-CHECKED FACT ABOUT
     THIS FILESYSTEM: single, mirror and striped-mirror profiles are what people run in
     production. THE PARITY PROFILES have carried a long-standing warning about the write hole
     from Series 1 · 15. That situation has been improving slowly for years, so DO NOT repeat
     a decade-old blog post — check the project's own status page for the version in Debian
     you are writing about, state the version, and if the answer is still "not recommended",
     say so plainly and move on.
     AND THE TRAP THAT MATTERS MOST TO THIS ACADEMY'S READER, which is genuinely data-specific
     and is why this episode earns its place: COPY-ON-WRITE FRAGMENTS FILES THAT ARE WRITTEN
     IN PLACE — a database's data directory, a VM image. The performance falls away over
     weeks, not immediately, so it looks like the database degrading.
     THE FIX IS `chattr +C` OR nodatacow — AND IT DISABLES CHECKSUMS FOR THOSE FILES. So the
     standard advice for putting Postgres on btrfs turns off the exact guarantee that was the
     reason to choose btrfs. Say that out loud. It is the cleanest example in the course of a
     mitigation that quietly removes the benefit, and a reader who understands it will ask the
     same question of every other tuning flag they are handed.
     WHEN TO CHOOSE IT: a Debian box where you want copy-on-write snapshots and checksums
     without a kernel module, mirrors are enough redundancy, and the workload is files rather
     than a database writing in place. That is a real and common shape.
